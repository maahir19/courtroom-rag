# Blue-Light-Filtering Glasses and Sleep Quality in University Students: A Crossover Trial

> Synthetic sample paper written for testing Courtroom RAG. The authors, data and journal are fictional.

Authors: K. Sharma, T. Nguyen, H. Abdi
Journal: Sleep and Behaviour Letters (fictional), 2024

## Abstract

University students often use screens late at night. We tested whether wearing blue-light-filtering glasses for three hours before bed improves sleep compared with clear placebo glasses. In a randomised crossover design, 64 students wore each pair for two weeks, with a one-week washout in between. Sleep was measured with wrist actigraphy and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI).

Sleep onset latency (time to fall asleep) was 4.2 minutes shorter with blue-light glasses (p = 0.04). Total sleep time and sleep efficiency did not differ significantly between conditions. Self-reported sleep quality improved slightly with the blue-light glasses, but participants could often guess which glasses they were wearing because of the amber tint.

## Methods detail

Glasses filtered 90% of light between 400 and 500 nm. Participants were asked to keep normal screen habits. Bedtime was not fixed. Caffeine use after 18:00 was recorded but not restricted.

## Results

Mean sleep onset latency: 21.6 minutes (blue-light glasses) vs 25.8 minutes (placebo).
Mean total sleep time: 6 h 41 min vs 6 h 37 min (p = 0.62).
Mean PSQI global score: 6.1 vs 6.9 (lower is better; p = 0.03).

Students who used their phones for more than three hours after 21:00 showed a larger improvement in sleep onset latency (7.9 minutes), while students with low evening screen use showed almost no change.

## Discussion

Blue-light-filtering glasses produced a small improvement in how quickly students fell asleep and in self-reported sleep quality. The effect on total sleep was negligible. Because the glasses were visibly tinted, a placebo effect cannot be ruled out for the self-reported outcomes.

The size of the effect was much smaller than that of behavioural changes reported in other studies, such as keeping a regular bedtime or stopping screen use 30 minutes before bed.

## Limitations

The sample was small and from a single university. Two weeks per condition may be too short to see changes in habitual sleep. Actigraphy estimates sleep from movement and is less accurate than polysomnography.
