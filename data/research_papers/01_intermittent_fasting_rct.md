# Time-Restricted Eating versus Daily Calorie Restriction for Weight Loss in Adults with Obesity: A 12-Month Randomised Trial

Authors: R. Menon, A. Kulkarni, S. Fernandes, L. Okafor
Journal: Journal of Applied Metabolic Research (fictional), 2025

## Abstract

Background: Time-restricted eating (TRE), a form of intermittent fasting where all food is eaten within a fixed window, is popular for weight loss. It is unclear whether it works better than conventional daily calorie restriction (CR) when both groups eat the same number of calories.

Methods: We randomly assigned 240 adults with obesity (BMI 30–40) to either TRE (eating between 08:00 and 16:00) with a 25% calorie deficit, or CR with the same 25% calorie deficit and no time restriction. The primary outcome was change in body weight at 12 months.

Results: Both groups lost weight. Mean weight loss at 12 months was 7.6 kg in the TRE group and 6.9 kg in the CR group. The difference of 0.7 kg was not statistically significant (95% CI −0.9 to 2.3; p = 0.38).

Conclusion: When calorie intake is matched, time-restricted eating did not produce significantly greater weight loss than daily calorie restriction over 12 months.

## Introduction

Intermittent fasting has gained wide attention on social media, with many claims that it "boosts metabolism" independently of calories. Earlier small studies reported promising results, but most compared fasting with no intervention at all, rather than with an equally intense calorie restriction diet.

## Results

Adherence was higher in the TRE group during the first three months: 82% of TRE participants met their eating window on at least five days per week, compared with 71% of CR participants who met their daily calorie target. However, adherence declined in both groups after six months and was similar at 12 months (61% vs 59%).

Participants in the TRE group reported less difficulty with "counting calories" and rated the diet as easier to follow on a 10-point scale (mean 6.8 vs 5.9).

Secondary outcomes, including waist circumference, fasting glucose, LDL cholesterol and blood pressure, improved in both groups with no significant difference between groups. Body fat percentage decreased by 3.1 points in TRE and 2.8 points in CR (not significant).

A subgroup analysis suggested that participants who ate dinner late in their previous routine lost slightly more weight with TRE, but this analysis was exploratory and not adjusted for multiple comparisons.

## Discussion

Our findings suggest that the main driver of weight loss is the calorie deficit itself rather than the timing of meals. Time-restricted eating may still be useful as a practical strategy, because some people find a fixed eating window easier to follow than counting calories.

## Limitations

The study was conducted at two centres in one country and most participants were aged 30–55. Calorie intake was self-reported using food diaries, which are known to underestimate intake. The early eating window (08:00–16:00) may not reflect how most people practise intermittent fasting, which usually involves skipping breakfast. Results may differ with later eating windows or with longer fasting periods such as alternate-day fasting.
