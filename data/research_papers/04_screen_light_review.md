## Background

Light in the blue part of the spectrum (around 460–480 nm) is the strongest signal for suppressing melatonin, the hormone that helps start sleep. Laboratory studies show that bright blue light in the evening can delay melatonin release by up to 90 minutes.

## How much light do phones actually emit?

Typical smartphone and laptop screens at normal brightness emit far less light than the laboratory sources used in melatonin studies. Field measurements suggest phone screens produce about 30–50 lux at eye level, compared with 500–1000 lux in many laboratory exposures. At these low levels, the melatonin delay from screens is usually estimated at 10–20 minutes.

## What else explains poor sleep in screen users?

Several studies indicate that the content of screen use matters as much as the light. Engaging content — gaming, social media scrolling, messaging — increases alertness and delays bedtime. Notifications during the night also fragment sleep. These effects are not reduced by blue-light filters.

## Evidence on blue-light filters and glasses

Results from trials of blue-blocking glasses are mixed. Some small trials report faster sleep onset, particularly in people with insomnia, delayed sleep phase, or heavy evening screen use. Larger and better-blinded trials generally find little or no effect on total sleep time in healthy adults.

A pooled analysis of 17 trials found no consistent improvement in sleep quality, sleep duration or daytime alertness with blue-light-filtering lenses, and rated the overall certainty of evidence as low.

## Practical recommendations

For most healthy people, reducing evening screen time, keeping a consistent sleep schedule and using "do not disturb" settings are likely to help sleep more than blue-light glasses. Blue-light glasses are low-risk and may provide a modest benefit for people who use bright screens for several hours before bed or who have delayed sleep phase.
