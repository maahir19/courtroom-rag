# Intermittent Fasting Regimens and Body Weight: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of 31 Trials

Authors: P. Iyer, M. Chen, D. Rossi
Journal: Nutrition Evidence Review (fictional), 2026

## Abstract

We pooled 31 randomised controlled trials (n = 2,684) comparing intermittent fasting (IF) regimens — time-restricted eating, alternate-day fasting and the 5:2 diet — with either usual diet or continuous calorie restriction.

Compared with usual diet, intermittent fasting reduced body weight by a mean of 4.3 kg (95% CI 3.5 to 5.1). Compared with continuous calorie restriction, intermittent fasting reduced body weight by an additional 0.6 kg (95% CI 0.1 to 1.1), a statistically significant but small difference.

## Results by regimen

Alternate-day fasting showed the largest effect versus calorie restriction (1.1 kg additional loss), while time-restricted eating showed the smallest (0.3 kg, not significant). The 5:2 diet was in between.

Dropout rates were higher for alternate-day fasting (24%) than for calorie restriction (17%), which suggests that the most effective regimen on paper may be the hardest to sustain.

## Quality of evidence

Most trials were short: 22 of the 31 trials lasted 12 weeks or less. Only four trials followed participants for a year or longer. Risk of bias was rated "some concerns" for 19 trials, mainly because participants and dietitians could not be blinded to the diet.

When we restricted the analysis to the four trials lasting at least 12 months, the difference between intermittent fasting and calorie restriction was 0.4 kg and no longer statistically significant.

## Interpretation

Intermittent fasting is an effective weight-loss approach compared with doing nothing, and it performs at least as well as continuous calorie restriction. The small extra benefit seen in short trials may be explained by better early adherence rather than a metabolic effect. We did not find good evidence that fasting "boosts metabolism"; resting energy expenditure did not differ between groups in the 9 trials that measured it.

## Safety

Adverse events were mostly mild, such as headaches, irritability and hunger in the first two weeks. People with diabetes on insulin or sulfonylureas experienced more episodes of low blood sugar during fasting days, so medical supervision is advised for this group. People with a history of eating disorders were excluded from almost all trials, so the safety of fasting for them is unknown.
